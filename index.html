Data-Driven Urban Systems & Spatial Analytics

Cities need tools that predict, monitor and model the system-level effects of infrastructure decisions. This work builds data pipelines that mine unstructured and open sources for new spatial insight, the kind of adaptive tools public agencies and private markets need.

1.1

Open-Data 3D City Modeling

Digital surface and terrain model differencing, and an open-data contribution pipeline

Type
Independent geospatial product, sole author
Date
June 2026
Stack
Python 3.11, WebGL, LiDAR raster processing (DSM/DEM), GitHub Actions

3D massing models drive density, shadow and real estate capacity analysis, but cities often choose between costly proprietary data and flat 2D records. In Auckland, OpenStreetMap footprints cover the city but mostly lack heights. A Python pipeline overlays the footprints on public municipal LiDAR to derive above-ground heights for 61,228 buildings, and renders the whole central city in a browser, with no software licenses needed.

1.2

A Time Series Estimate of Protected Lane Returns on Citi Bike Demand

What 50 miles a year of protected bike lanes does to ridership

Type
Independent study, Urban Systems Engineering Lab, Columbia
Supervisor
Prof. Markus Schläpfer
Date
Sept.–Dec. 2024

The NYC Streets Plan requires at least 50 miles of protected bike lanes a year. I built a weekly 2022–2024 panel from Citi Bike trip records, weather data and Transportation Alternatives' lane tracker, then estimated the effect of lane expansion on ridership with regression models that have autoregressive errors. Each additional mile of bike lane was associated with about 116 more Citi Bike trips a day.

2.1

Reimagine Manhattan: A Mathematical Modeling Approach for Superblock Development

Graph models of the street network to find where superblocks would work

Type
Collaborative research, Columbia Summer Undergraduate Research in Mathematical Modeling
Supervisor
Prof. George Dragomir
Date
June–Aug. 2024; ongoing
Presented
Columbia Fall Research Symposium 2024; Jane Street Math Day 2024

A graph-theoretic model combines Manhattan's street network with points of interest to score superblock suitability and simulate a seeded growth strategy for pilots, aimed at traffic calming, safety, public space and small business. I led the project definition and methodology, built the suitability model, made the visualizations, and did fieldwork in Barcelona in August 2025 to see how superblocks work day to day.

3.1

Climate Resiliency Modeling: Mobility Under Extreme Weather

The June 2023 wildfire haze as a natural experiment

Type
Collaborative, Sustainable Urban Systems Engineering
Supervisor
Prof. Markus Schläpfer
Date
Feb.–May 2025

News coverage suggested many New Yorkers kept traveling despite health advisories during the June 2023 haze. Using bikeshare, subway, bus and taxi data aligned with daily AQI and PM2.5, plus regressions controlling for season and weather, the project tests how travel actually changed by mode and neighborhood. I led the concept and methods and built the Citi Bike origin–destination analysis in Python.

3.4

Spatial Regression: Rethinking Proximity and Risk in San Francisco

Who lives closest to flood risk, and what is it worth

Type
Collaborative, Advanced GIS
Supervisor
Prof. Leah Meisterlin
Date
Feb.–May 2025

Block-group data with elevation, horizontal and combined 3D distance to flood zones. Little support for the idea that lower-income areas sit closer to flood risk; the most valuable land is often closest, along the waterfront. That raises a moral-hazard question: coastal protection may draw more high-value development into risky areas.

3.6

Housing Policy Alignment Analytics Using Sentiment Methods

126 years of New York Times coverage of Lower East Side housing

Type
Sole author
Date
2026
Methods
NYT Archive API, VADER with custom lexicons, frame classification, Bai–Perron structural breaks

10,118 New York Times articles (1900–April 2026) on housing in Manhattan and the Lower East Side, scored for sentiment with domain lexicons and drift-aware scoring, then tested for structural breaks in how the paper framed public housing.
